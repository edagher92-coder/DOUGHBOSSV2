## 1. Australian Consumer Law: misleading or deceptive conduct and substantiation

Plain-English rule

- OBSERVED: Section 18 of the Australian Consumer Law (ACL) says a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive (Competition and Consumer Act 2010, Sch 2, s 18, compilation 2026-05-27).
- OBSERVED: Section 29(1) prohibits false or misleading representations, in connection with the supply or promotion of goods or services, about things such as standard, quality, value, grade and composition, testimonials, sponsorship, approval or affiliation, price, and place of origin of goods (s 29(1)(a) to (k), and further limbs).
- OBSERVED: The ACCC says "A business must be able to prove any claim they advertise", that intent is irrelevant, and that it applies to advertising, packaging, quotes, staff statements, social media, testimonials and websites. It lists as breaches false price promotions, contradictory fine print, bait advertising, unsubstantiated "premium or benefit" claims and future predictions without reasonable grounds. Businesses should "check that the overall general impression is accurate". Source: https://www.accc.gov.au/business/advertising-and-promotions/false-or-misleading-claims (retrieved 2026-10-02).
- OBSERVED: Section 35 (bait advertising) prohibits advertising goods or services at a specified price if there are reasonable grounds to believe you cannot supply them at that price for a reasonable period and in reasonable quantities.
- INFERRED: Section 18 applies "in trade or commerce", so it covers business-to-business marketing to corporate buyers as well as consumer ads. A corporate catering page is not exempt.

Claims Dough Boss is likely to make, and what each needs (INFERRED application)

| Claim | Risk | What to have before it goes live |
|---|---|---|
| "Best", "number one", "the best Lebanese bakery in Sydney" | A ranking or comparison claim is a statement of fact if read literally; general puffery is judged by the overall impression, and I did not find an ACCC page that defines a safe puffery line | Prefer a specific, provable statement ("baked on site each morning") over "best". If "best" is used, hold a basis (a named award with date, a review count). LAWYER for any comparative claim against a named competitor |
| "Authentic" / "traditional Lebanese" | Ambiguous; could imply recipe origin, ingredient origin or maker background | Say what is true and specific (recipe source, how it is made); keep a record of the basis |

If any item uses a frozen component, restrict the claim to the items it is true for. Keep a claims ledger (see checklist) |
| "Halal" | FSANZ says the Food Standards Code has no halal labelling requirements and that halal claims are subject to consumer law (EXTRACT, https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/consumer/labelling/Labelling-consumervalueissues). If a "made in Australia" claim is used, confirm the ingredients and process support it |
| Vegan, vegetarian, dairy-free | No Food Standards Code definition found in what I read | Treat as factual claims: check recipes, shared fryers, glazes and cross-contact. See section 8 |

What this means for our copy and campaigns

- Every claim on any page, ad or email must map to a row in a claims ledger with the evidence and the date confirmed. Unconfirmed claims stay out or carry a `[CONFIRM]` marker in drafts.
- Write specific facts, not superlatives. A teaser such as "Something exciting is coming" is a statement about a future matter: hold a dated record of what is actually planned before it is published, and use "early access" only if list members will really get it (section 12).
- Fine print must not contradict the headline.

Flag: LAWYER for any comparative claim, any health or "better for you" claim, and the exact wording of "halal".

## 2. Price display, indicative prices and bait advertising

Plain-English rule

- OBSERVED: Section 48 (single price) says a person must not, in connection with supplying or promoting goods or services "of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use or consumption", make a representation about an amount that would be part of the consideration unless they also specify, in a prominent way and as a single figure, the single price. A carve-out in s 48(2) and (3) deals with postage charges: if not included, a known minimum must be specified (compilation 2026-05-27).
- OBSERVED: The ACL definition of "consumer" (s 3) counts a buyer of goods as a consumer where the amount paid did not exceed $40,000 (or a greater prescribed amount), or the goods were of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use or consumption, with a re-supply carve-out.
- OBSERVED: Section 29(1)(i) prohibits a false or misleading representation about the price of goods or services; section 35 covers bait advertising (see section 1 above).
- OBSERVED: A search of the 2026-05-27 text for the term "drip" found no match. INFERRED: no express "drip pricing" provision was in that compilation. A Treasury consultation on unfair trading practices appears in search (an OAIC submission page), so a new rule may be coming; its status is not verified. Check before any pricing campaign.
- OBSERVED: The ACCC's guidance page on displaying prices at `/business/pricing-surcharges-and-offers/displaying-prices` returned HTTP 404 on 2026-10-02, so I could not read the ACCC's practical guidance.

What this means for our copy and campaigns (INFERRED)

- Food is a kind of good ordinarily acquired for personal consumption, so catering menus aimed at the public likely engage the single-price rule. Corporate quotes could be treated either way; LAWYER to confirm.
- The three package prices on the Dough Boss catering page are labelled INDICATIVE by the owner. Do not show them in ads, snippets or structured data as firm prices. A page may say "indicative, confirmed in your written quote", but the headline price and the fine print must agree.
- If a price is advertised, state whether it includes GST, delivery, set-up and any surcharge in the same place, as a single figure where s 48 applies.
- If a price promotion is run, do not advertise a quantity or price the stores cannot honour (bait rule). Set a stock or capacity limit in the offer.
- Google Ads separately requires disclosure of "the payment model or full expense that a user will bear" (see section 10).

Flag: LAWYER on whether corporate catering quotes fall under s 48, GST-inclusive display for business buyers, and any upcoming unfair-trading-practices reforms.

## 3. Testimonials, reviews and fake reviews

Plain-English rule

- OBSERVED: ACL s 29(1)(e) and (f) prohibit false or misleading representations that purport to be a testimonial, or concerning a testimonial.
- OBSERVED: The ACCC says businesses must not create fake reviews or arrange others to; incentivised reviews are a breach unless the incentive applies equally to positive and negative reviews and is clearly disclosed; reviews must not be suppressed or edited (it cites a $2.9 million penalty in the HealthEngine matter); reviews from undisclosed family, employees or paid contributors must not be presented as genuine; total review counts should be shown so star ratings have context. Source: https://www.accc.gov.au/business/advertising-and-promotions/online-reviews (retrieved 2026-10-02).
- OBSERVED: Google's Maps contributed-content policy bars offering "payment, discounts, free goods and/or services - in exchange for posting any review", pressuring users to leave reviews on the premises, and asking staff to "solicit a certain number of reviews". It does allow soliciting or encouraging genuine reviews "without offering incentives" (https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114). Google's guidance on asking for reviews says businesses can ask customers to visit a link or scan a QR code, and bars incentives (https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122).
- OBSERVED: The page I read on Google's review guidance does not address "review gating" (only inviting happy customers). INFERRED: selectively asking only satisfied customers risks both the ACCC's point about suppression and a platform rule I could not read; do not do it.

What this means for our copy and campaigns

- No "leave a five-star review for 10 per cent off" and no free tray for a review. Do not tie any catering discount to a review.
- Ask every customer the same way, with a QR code or link; reply to negative reviews honestly.
- Never quote a testimonial unless it is real, attributable with permission and still current. Anonymous or invented corporate quotes are not allowed.
- Staff, family and friends reviewing the stores is a disclosure and policy risk; do not do it.
- Show the real rating and review count when quoting a star rating.

Flag: LAWYER if any loyalty program or catering rebate ever mentions reviews.

## 4. Spam Act 2003: email, SMS and other electronic marketing

Plain-English rule (OBSERVED from the Act text unless marked EXTRACT)

- A commercial electronic message is one whose purpose, or one of whose purposes, is to offer to supply or to advertise or promote goods, services or a supplier of them (s 6(1)(d) to (f)). A catering offer by email or SMS is a commercial electronic message. The OAIC page says the Act covers "electronic messages (email, SMS, instant messages)" (https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/direct-marketing).
- Consent (s 16): a person must not send a commercial electronic message with an Australian link unless the recipient consented, and the message is not a "designated" one (s 16(1) and (2)). Consent is express consent or consent that can reasonably be inferred from conduct and business or other relationships (Sch 2, clause 2).
- Published addresses (Sch 2, clause 4): consent "may not be inferred from the mere fact that the relevant electronic address has been published". The exception applies if the address enables the public to send messages to a particular employee, director, officer, partner, office-holder or self-employed individual, or to an individual or group performing a particular function or role in an organisation; and the address has been conspicuously published; and it would be reasonable to assume publication was with the agreement of the person (or the organisation, for a role); and the publication is not accompanied by a statement that the account-holder does not want unsolicited commercial messages. Then consent is taken to exist "so long as the messages are relevant to" the person's work-related business, functions or duties, or the office, position or function concerned.
- Sender identification (s 17): the message must clearly and accurately identify the individual or organisation that authorised it, with accurate contact information that is likely to remain valid for at least 30 days.
- Unsubscribe (s 18): a functional unsubscribe statement presented "in a clear and conspicuous manner", to an address likely to receive unsubscribe messages for at least 30 days after sending. EXTRACT (ACMA via search, https://www.acma.gov.au/avoid-sending-spam): businesses "must generally stop sending you marketing within 5 business days" of an unsubscribe request, and ACMA treats inferred consent as covering a provable ongoing relationship, not "sending messages after someone has just bought something".
- Address harvesting (Part 3, ss 19 to 22, titles read): the Act prohibits supplying, acquiring or using address-harvesting software and harvested-address lists. OBSERVED from the table of contents; I did not read the detailed sections.
- Penalties: the Act has civil-penalty provisions (ss 24 and 25) and formal warnings; I did not retrieve the dollar amounts. "Not publicly available" in what I read; check ACMA.

How this applies to business email addresses and SMS (INFERRED)

- B2B email is not exempt. A message to an office address is still a commercial electronic message. The route most relevant to cold outreach is the conspicuous-publication exception, and it is narrow: the address must be public, linked to a person or role, with no "no unsolicited marketing" statement, and the message must be relevant to that person's or role's work. A catering offer to a facilities manager, executive assistant or events coordinator address is arguably relevant; the same offer to a generic accountant address or to an unrelated role is weaker. INFERRED: personal-looking addresses of individuals are riskier than clearly role-based ones. REGULATOR for any doubtful list.
- Record the evidence: for each recipient, store the URL where the address was published, the date, and the role. Without evidence, the sender bears the burden when relying on a defence (the Act places an evidential burden on a person who relies on certain exceptions, s 16(5)).
- Do not scrape pages in bulk, buy lists of addresses or use harvesting tools (Part 3). Build lists one by one from clearly published role addresses, or from people who opt in.
- SMS follows the same consent, sender and unsubscribe rules. Use express opt-in for any customer SMS, with sender name and an unsubscribe route that works.
- A sign-up form on the catering page should offer express consent, name the business as sender, and say what will be sent. Keep the consent record (time, source, wording).
- Every email needs the legal name or trading name of Dough Boss's operating entity (confirm the entity name and ABN publicly `[CONFIRM]`), a working contact route, and a one-click or reply-based unsubscribe.

What this means for our copy and campaigns

- Cold B2B email sequences: role-addressed, relevant, evidence-recorded, with unsubscribe and sender block; stop on request within the ACMA timeframe (EXTRACT: 5 business days).
- No purchased lists. No bulk scraping.
- Newsletter and the generic "coming soon" waitlist: express opt-in, double opt-in recommended, and no pre-ticked boxes (pre-ticked boxes are my recommendation, not a statement in the sources I read). The waitlist form names the sender and links to the privacy policy at the point of collection.

Flag: REGULATOR (ACMA) for the exact current B2B consent test and the unsubscribe timeframe; the current Spam Act compilation and Spam Regulations must be checked, because I read the as-made 2003 text.

## 5. Do Not Call Register and telemarketing

Plain-English rule

- OBSERVED: The Do Not Call Register Act 2006 says a person must not make, or cause to be made, a telemarketing call to an Australian number registered on the Register, unless the call is a designated telemarketing call (s 11(1)), subject to consent (s 11(2)). Source: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2006A00088/2021-09-01/2021-09-01/text/original/pdf.
- OBSERVED: A "business number" is defined as an Australian number other than one used or maintained "exclusively or primarily for private or domestic purposes". A number is eligible for registration only if it is used or maintained primarily for private or domestic purposes, or exclusively for faxes.
- OBSERVED: The Do Not Call Register consumer FAQ says business telephone numbers cannot be registered, that mixed-use numbers can be registered if "mostly for private/domestic purposes", that any call with "a sales offer, advertisement or promotion" is a telemarketing call even if secondary, and that registered charities are exempt (https://www.donotcall.gov.au/consumers/faqs-for-consumers).
- EXTRACT (ACMA via search): ACMA "cannot accept complaints about telemarketing calls to business telephone numbers, unless the caller has failed to meet the requirements of the Industry Standard". I did not read the Telemarketing and Research Calls Industry Standard itself, so calling hours, caller identification and call-ending requirements are not verified here. REGULATOR.

What this means for our copy and campaigns (INFERRED)

- The Register does not stop calls to numbers that are business numbers, which includes the published landline of an office. That is not permission to call freely: the Industry Standard still applies, and mobile numbers and sole-trader numbers may be mixed-use and registered. Wash any list of mobiles or sole-trader numbers against the Register using the official washing service (fees and process not retrieved).
- Dough Boss's plan is email and web led. If cold calling is added, read the Industry Standard first, state who is calling, and stop when asked.
- Calls to consumers (households, parent committees, personal mobiles) must be washed against the Register.

Flag: REGULATOR (ACMA) before any outbound calling campaign.

## 6. Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs)

Plain-English rule

- OBSERVED: There are 13 APPs. The relevant ones are APP 3 (collection), APP 5 (notification), APP 6 (use and disclosure), APP 7 (direct marketing), APP 8 (cross-border disclosure) and APP 11 (security) (https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-quick-reference).
- OBSERVED: The OAIC defines a small business as turnover of $3 million or less. Small businesses are generally outside the Act unless an exception applies (for example trading in personal information, health service providers, certain contractors) and "can voluntarily join". APP 7 applies to organisations with turnover above $3 million and some small businesses (https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/small-business; direct marketing page).
- OBSERVED: APP 7 applies only where the Spam Act and Do Not Call Register Act do not; those take priority. Marketing based on information collected from the individual is allowed if the person would reasonably expect it and a simple opt-out is provided; sensitive information needs explicit consent for direct marketing. Unfair collection is not allowed.
- OBSERVED: On tracking pixels (Meta Pixel, Google tags), the OAIC says organisations should apply data minimisation, notify individuals about third-party pixels, take reasonable steps under APP 8 where pixel providers send data overseas, not send sensitive information to pixels without opt-in, update privacy policies and review configurations regularly (https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/tracking-pixels-and-privacy-obligations).
- Turnover of Dough Boss: not publicly available. INFERRED: with three stores and a growing catering line, the business may or may not exceed $3 million. Ask the accountant. I did not verify whether the small business exemption has been narrowed by recent reforms; the OAIC page I read said nothing on reform. LAWYER.

What this means for our copy and campaigns (INFERRED)

- Even if Dough Boss is exempt, Google Customer Match requires advertisers to disclose in their privacy policy that they share customer data with third parties and to obtain consent where required (https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6299717), and Meta restricts transferring Meta data (https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/). Marketing tools and the Spam Act and ACL apply regardless of turnover. Treat the APPs as the standard.
- Publish a privacy policy that names the data collected (name, business, email, phone, event details), the purposes, the platforms that receive it (Google, Meta, an email provider) and that some of them are overseas.
- Lead forms: collect the minimum. A catering enquiry needs name, organisation, role, email or phone, date, headcount and dietary counts. Free-text dietary and health needs can reveal health or religious information, which is sensitive information needing explicit consent. Ask for dietary needs as counts and categories at the quote stage, with a note about why it is collected, and do not send that field to ad platforms.
- Do not upload customer lists, event enquiry details or dietary data to ad platforms without consent and a privacy-policy disclosure. Do not send form content to the Meta Pixel or Google tag.
- Implement consent mode and cookie notices as a platform requirement and good practice; the OAIC page I read recommends notice and opt-out for targeted advertising.

Flag: LAWYER on the small business exemption, the content of the privacy policy and overseas disclosure wording.

## 7. Food allergens, labelling and what catering marketing may say

Plain-English rule

- OBSERVED: Under the Food Standards Code, from 25 February 2024 allergens must be declared using required names: wheat, fish, crustacean, mollusc, egg, milk, lupin, peanut, soy, sesame, individual tree nuts and sulphites at 10 mg/kg or more. Packaged food needs the declaration in the ingredient list and a bold "contains" summary. For food not required to bear a label, such as at a cafe or takeaway, allergen information must be displayed with the food or provided on request, using the required names (https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/business/labelling/allergen-labelling).
- OBSERVED: "May contain" statements are voluntary and not regulated by the Code. Businesses must ensure food is safe and suitable, with training for food handlers under Standard 3.2.2A (https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/business/food-safety/allergen-management).
- EXTRACT: A "gluten free" claim requires that the food contain no detectable gluten (and no oats or malted gluten cereals); "low gluten" means no more than 20 mg per 100 g (FSANZ Standard 1.2.7 and guidance via https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/sites/default/files/food-standards-code/proposals/Documents/P293%20FAR%20Short%20Guide.pdf).
- EXTRACT: Halal is not regulated by the Code; halal claims are subject to consumer law (see section 1).
- EXTRACT: The AANA Food and Beverages Advertising Code (from 1 November 2021) defines children as under 15, restricts advertising of occasional foods targeting children, and bars promotions that create a sense of urgency or encourage excessive consumption (https://aana.com.au/self-regulation/food-and-beverages-code/). Whether this self-regulatory code binds Dough Boss is not established; follow it as best practice. INFERRED relevance: party and family-event food, and any teaser or new product line, could be read as aimed at families.

What this means for our copy and campaigns (INFERRED)

- A catering menu should carry an allergen matrix per item using the exact allergen names, and a statement of how to request more detail. Bakeries handle wheat, sesame, milk, egg and nuts in shared spaces, so do not promise "allergen-free".
- Treat "gluten-free" as a regulated claim. A bakery that mills and handles wheat flour may not be able to show "no detectable gluten" for a product made in the same space. Prefer wording such as "made without gluten-containing ingredients" only if true, plus a cross-contact notice, and never target coeliac customers with a claim that cannot be proven. LAWYER and REGULATOR (NSW Food Authority, not read here) before any gluten-free marketing.
- Vegan and vegetarian claims: confirm recipes and glazes (for example egg wash, dairy and gelatine) and cross-contact.
- No health or nutrition claims such as "healthy" or "high protein" without checking Standard 1.2.7 (I did not read its health-claim rules; REGULATOR).
- Party food, teasers and any new product line: speak to parents and organisers; avoid cartoon-led imagery aimed at children and urgency language; do not offer food as prizes to children.

Flag: REGULATOR (NSW Food Authority and FSANZ) for allergen, gluten-free and food-service obligations in NSW; this research did not read NSW Food Act material.

## 8. B2B catering terms: unfair contract terms

Plain-English rule

- EXTRACT: From 9 November 2023, the ACL prohibits unfair terms in standard form contracts with consumers and small businesses, with penalties; small business means under 100 employees or turnover under $10 million; penalties for companies are the greater of $50 million, three times the benefit or 30 per cent of turnover (https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/accc-welcomes-new-penalties-and-expansion-of-the-unfair-contract-terms-laws).

What this means (INFERRED)

- A standard catering terms sheet sent to corporate and small-business customers (cancellation fees, deposits, price variation, short-notice headcount changes, one-sided liability limits) is a standard form contract. Keep terms reasonable, transparent and mutual. LAWYER should review the catering T&Cs before launch.

## 9. Google Business Profile guidelines

Plain-English rule (OBSERVED, https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177, retrieved 2026-10-02)

- Business name: must "reflect your business's real-world name, as used consistently on your storefront, website, stationery". No marketing taglines, store codes, trademark symbols, phone numbers, URLs or fully capitalised words.
- Categories: select the fewest necessary; choose categories that complete "This business IS a", not what it has or offers; do not use categories as keywords.
- Description: no "low-quality, irrelevant, or distracting content", no focus on sales promotions, no links.
- Service-area businesses: need one profile for a central office with a defined service area and cannot list a virtual office unless staffed in business hours. Eligibility needs a physical location customers can visit or a business that travels to customers.
- Reviews and incentives: see section 3.

What this means for our copy and campaigns (INFERRED)

- Create or claim one profile per store, each with the store's real name and address. The name must be "Dough Boss" as used on signs; do not stuff it with "Corporate Catering Bankstown".
- Pick a primary category that says what each store is (for example a bakery); add catering as a secondary category or a service only if it matches the guideline wording. Check the category list in the dashboard.
- Do not create a separate "Dough Boss Catering" profile at a fake or unstaffed address; the guidelines bar virtual offices without staffed hours. A catering delivery service-area profile is not necessary unless there is a staffed catering office.
- Keep the description factual; no discount text or links.

Flag: REGULATOR (Google) for category and attribute availability, which change over time.

## 10. Advertising platform policies

Google Ads (OBSERVED, retrieved 2026-10-02)

- Misrepresentation: "Making inaccurate claims or claims that entice the user with an improbable result" is not allowed; "Failure to clearly and conspicuously disclose the payment model or full expense that a user will bear before and after purchase is not allowed"; do not hide or misrepresent information about the business or products; do not use clickbait or sensationalist text or imagery (https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6020955).

## 11. Pre-publication checklist (run on any ad, email, page or post)

Copy and claims

1. Does every factual claim map to a row in the claims ledger with evidence and a confirmed date? Any claim marked `[CONFIRM]` is removed or cleared first.
2. No unsupported superlatives ("best", "number one", "authentic") and no absolutes ("never", "always", "100 per cent") unless proven for every product shown.
3. "Fresh", "baked in-house", "never frozen": has Elie confirmed this for the exact product shown (including catering lines and any new product line)?
4. "Halal": removed unless the exact basis is confirmed and, if certified, the certifier is named and current.
5. No health, nutrition, "healthy" or gluten-free claim unless checked against FSANZ Standard 1.2.7 and cleared (see section 7).
6. Delivery area, lead time, minimum order and set-up claims are confirmed and published as stated.
7. Fine print does not contradict the headline; the overall impression is accurate.
8. Dates, prices and quantities are verified against the live price list. Indicative prices are labelled and not used as firm headline prices.

Price and offer

9. One prominent single price where required; GST, delivery and surcharges stated in the same place.
10. Offer can be honoured in quantity and time (no bait).
11. No promotion tied to a review.

Testimonials and reviews

12. Every testimonial is real, current and used with permission; no invented, staff, family or paid reviews.
13. Star ratings shown with the real review count.

Email, SMS and calls

14. Recipient has express consent or a documented conspicuous-publication basis (URL, date, role) and the message is relevant to that role.
15. Message names the sender and gives working contact details; has a clear unsubscribe that works for at least 30 days; unsubscribes are honoured promptly (EXTRACT: within 5 business days).
16. No purchased or scraped list. No address-harvesting tools.
17. Phone lists: mobiles and sole-trader numbers washed against the Do Not Call Register; Industry Standard read before calling.

Privacy and data

18. Privacy policy is live, names the data collected, the purposes and overseas platforms.
19. Lead forms collect the minimum; dietary data is collected as counts and kept out of ad platform and pixel payloads.
20. Pixels and tags fire per the consent choice; customer lists are uploaded only with disclosure and consent.

Platform and audience

21. No religion, ethnicity or other personal-attribute language or targeting in ad copy.
22. Party, teaser and new-product ads speak to parents and organisers, not children; no urgency or excess-consumption language. A teaser names no product and makes no claim about it (`docs/site/teaser-direction.md`).
23. Google Business Profile name, category and description follow the guidelines.
24. Ad copy and landing page match; no clickbait.

Allergens and contracts

25. Allergen information is available using the Code's required names, with a matrix for catering menus; no "allergen-free" promise.
26. Catering terms have been reviewed for unfair-contract-term risk before sending to businesses.

Sign-off

27. A second person has read the item against this list. For any item flagged LAWYER or REGULATOR above, written clearance is on file before launch.
